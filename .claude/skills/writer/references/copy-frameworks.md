# Copy frameworks — the canon

Ten patterns for headlines and landing-page copy. Two rules that decide if a
line of copy has earned its place. Seven psychological biases doing the real
work underneath. This is the reference; the SKILL.md file is the craft.

**Use one framework per page.** Don't mix. A page that opens with PAS and
shifts to BAB three sections in reads like two writers handed off mid-draft.
Pick the pattern that fits the buyer's state of mind and commit.

---

## How to choose

Before reaching for a template, answer three questions about the reader:

1. **What do they already believe?** — A buyer who hasn't named the pain
   needs PAS. A buyer who's been promised the moon by competitors needs
   plain truth or numbered nouns. A buyer comparing alternatives needs
   Dunford-style positioning.
2. **What do they not yet believe?** — The headline's job is to close the
   gap between the belief they walked in with and the belief they need to
   click. Frameworks are routes between two points; pick the one whose
   start matches where they are.
3. **What can you prove?** — Outcome + timeframe only works if you can
   prove the speed. Hard-thing-without-pain only works if removal is real.
   A framework you can't back with proof becomes a hype line.

If you can't answer all three, you aren't ready to pick a framework. Go
back to Step 0 of the skill (audience, medium, goal).

## The ten frameworks

### 1. JTBD — "Jobs to be done"

**Template:** `{Product} helps {audience} {job} without {friction}`

**When:** Mature category. Buyer knows the category exists; the question is
which tool. The job is clear, the friction is felt.

**Why it works:** The buyer hires products to do jobs (Christensen). This
template names the job in their language and removes the obstacle in one
line. No category education needed.

**Examples that work:**
- "Linear helps software teams ship faster without process overhead."
- "Stripe helps internet businesses accept payments without becoming
  payments experts."

**Examples that fail:**
- "Our platform helps modern enterprises optimise their workflows." — no
  specific job, no specific friction, no audience tighter than "modern
  enterprises". Anyone could put their logo on this.

**Trap:** Generic audience + generic job + generic friction = generic
headline. The whole framework lives or dies on specificity. "Marketers"
is not an audience. "B2B SaaS marketers at companies under 50 people"
might be.

### 2. PAS — Problem · Agitate · Solve

**Template:**
1. Name the pain in the buyer's words.
2. Amplify the cost — what does the pain *actually cost* (time, money, sleep)?
3. Deliver the relief.

**When:** The pain is felt but unsolved, or solved badly. The buyer has
the problem but hasn't bought a solution because they've underestimated
the cost or no one has named it properly.

**Why it works:** Loss aversion. Naming the cost makes the status quo
visible as a *decision* rather than a default. Once the status quo costs
something, the choice changes.

**Example:**
> Your team ships three features a quarter. Your competitor ships nine.
> While you debate priorities, they ship the feature your customer
> just asked for. **Linear cuts the loop between decision and ship.**

**Trap:** Over-agitate and you sound like an infomercial. The amplification
should be specific and true. If you can't name the cost in numbers or in a
scene the buyer recognises, the agitation falls flat.

---

### 3. BAB — Before · After · Bridge

**Template:**
1. Before: the old world (with the buyer in it, suffering quietly).
2. After: the new world (with the buyer in it, vindicated).
3. Bridge: the mechanism — how you get from Before to After.

**When:** Category shift. You're not making the buyer faster at what they
do; you're moving them to a different way of doing it. Transformation
story, not optimisation story.

**Why it works:** The contrast does the persuasion. The Before scene gives
the buyer permission to admit they're in it. The After gives them
something to want. The Bridge gives them a path that doesn't feel like a
leap.

**Example (Superhuman, ~2018):**
- Before: Email runs your day. You're always behind.
- After: You hit Inbox Zero by 9am and get on with your work.
- Bridge: Keyboard-first email designed for speed. Average user replies
  in half the time.

**Trap:** Asymmetric scenes. If Before is concrete and After is abstract
("you'll feel productive and empowered"), the line breaks. Both scenes
need the same texture.

---

### 4. Outcome + timeframe

**Template:** `{Outcome} in {time}`

**When:** You can prove the speed. The buyer's biggest objection is "this
will take forever to roll out / learn / show value", and you have the
receipts to disprove it.

**Why it works:** Specificity is persuasion. A number anchors. A
timeframe is a promise the buyer can hold you to — which is exactly why
buyers trust it.

**Examples that work:**
- "Accept crypto in 60 seconds." (pay.one.ie)
- "From signup to first deploy in 4 minutes." (Vercel-style)
- "Hire your first engineer in 7 days." (recruiting platform)

**Examples that fail:**
- "Get value faster." — no number, no time, no proof.
- "10x your productivity." — number with no unit, "10x" is now a tell.

**Trap:** If the timeframe is real for 30% of users and aspirational for
the rest, eventually it shows up in churn. Don't promise speed you can't
hit for the median buyer.

---

### 5. Hard thing without pain

**Template:** `{Outcome} without {usual cost}`

**When:** Differentiation is *removal*. Every competitor has the feature;
you're the one who doesn't have the friction.

**Why it works:** The buyer already wants the outcome — that's the
category. The "without" line is what makes you different inside the
category. Removal is more persuasive than addition because removal is
rarer.

**Examples that work:**
- "Touch ID signs every transaction — no passwords, no recovery
  phrases."
- "Bookkeeping without spreadsheets."
- "Hire globally without setting up entities."

**Trap:** The "without" has to be a real removal the buyer has felt as
friction. "Accept crypto without complexity" is meaningless because
"complexity" isn't a specific cost. "Accept crypto without writing a line
of code" is — code is a specific cost the buyer can picture.

---

### 6. Plain truth

**Template:** One declarative sentence. No decoration.

**When:** The truth is good enough. The product does something
remarkable, the audience already knows the category, and dressing it up
would dilute it.

**Why it works:** Decoration signals weakness. A product confident enough
to state itself flatly signals strength. Plain truth also reads as
honesty — a rare currency in marketing.

**Examples that work:**
- "We make the iPhone." — Apple in an alternate universe.
- "Type less. Ship more." — Cursor, roughly.
- "Your wallet, on your phone. Touch ID to sign."

**Trap:** Plain truth requires a truth that's actually plainly good. If
you have to choose between "the only crypto wallet that uses Apple's
Secure Enclave" and "a powerful wallet for the modern web", you don't
have a plain truth — you have hype dressed plainly.

---

### 7. Numbered nouns

**Template:** `{N} {things}. {result}.`

**When:** Architecture or system products. The buyer needs to grasp the
shape of the thing, not just the benefit. Useful for technical audiences
who reward precision over polish.

**Why it works:** Numbers chunk. "Six dimensions, seven loops, six verbs,
four outcomes" gives a developer a mental scaffolding they can hang
details on. It also signals: this is designed, not assembled.

**Examples that work:**
- "Six dimensions. Seven loops. One substrate." (ONE)
- "Two roots. One biometric. Paper resurrects." (mac.md)
- "5 routes. 1 wallet. Touch ID signs everything." (one.ie)

**Trap:** The numbers have to mean something. If "6 features, 10
integrations, 24/7 support" reads as a checklist, it's not numbered
nouns — it's a brochure.

---

### 8. Category for audience

**Template:** `The {category} for {audience}`

**When:** You're carving a sub-category. The general category exists and
is crowded; your move is to claim a slice of it by audience.

**Why it works:** Categories are how buyers shortlist. If you can name a
new one and own it, you've removed every general-category competitor
from the comparison. The audience qualifier is the wedge.

**Examples that work:**
- "The CRM for founders." (vs. Salesforce for everyone)
- "The bank for freelancers." (vs. high-street banks)
- "The wallet for people who don't want to read a whitepaper."

**Trap:** The audience has to be one a buyer self-identifies with
*before* they read your page. "The platform for forward-thinking
enterprises" fails because no buyer has ever introduced themselves as a
forward-thinking enterprise.

---

### 9. April Dunford positioning

**Template:** `Unlike {alternative}, {capability} so {value}`

**When:** Competitive market. The buyer is actively comparing. They will
hear about you in the same breath as someone else, so the positioning
has to do the contrast for them.

**Why it works:** It names the alternative (which the buyer was already
thinking about), states the capability that makes you different, and
ladders to the value that capability creates. It does the buyer's
homework for them.

**Example:**
> Unlike legacy CRMs built for sales managers, Attio is built around
> your data model, so the CRM bends to your business instead of the
> other way round.

**Trap:** Punching down. If you name an alternative that's beneath you,
you give them oxygen. Name the alternative the buyer is most likely to
choose over you — that's the comparison worth winning.

---

### 10. Real attributed quote

**Template:** A customer or thinker said it best. Use their words. Attribute.

**When:** The quote is *that* good. A user described the product better
than you ever will, or a respected voice in the buyer's world endorsed
the category.

**Why it works:** Authority + social proof, stacked. Your headline is
someone else's testimony, which means it doesn't read as marketing — it
reads as evidence.

**Example (Stripe, paraphrased from their early site):**
> "Stripe is by far the easiest payments setup I've used. We were taking
> payments in an hour." — Patrick Collison's customer, attributed.

**Trap:** Quote stuffed with hedges. "I think Stripe is probably one of
the better payments platforms we've evaluated" is not a headline. If the
quote has any hedge in it, find a different quote or use a different
framework.

## The two rules every headline has to pass

### The specificity rule (Harry Dry)

> If a competitor could put their logo on your headline and have it
> still be true, rewrite.

This is the single test that catches most weak marketing. Run every
headline through it.

- "We help businesses grow." — Anyone. Rewrite.
- "Enterprise-grade security." — Anyone selling to enterprise. Rewrite.
- "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio." — Only New Balance.
  Ships.

The rule applies to subheads, feature names, and CTAs too. "Get started"
fails it. "Mint your wallet" passes it.

A practical drill: write your headline. Below it, paste the headline
from your three biggest competitors. If yours could be theirs or theirs
could be yours, none of you are differentiated. The first to rewrite
wins.

### The sensory rule

Concrete nouns are recalled at roughly **+200%** the rate of abstract
concepts (psycholinguistic research on the *concreteness effect*).
Pictures, things, and verbs of physical action live in memory. Concepts
fade.

- Abstract: "industry-leading security model."
- Concrete: "Touch ID signs every transaction."

- Abstract: "we'll save you time."
- Concrete: "ship Friday's release on Thursday afternoon."

- Abstract: "AI-powered insights."
- Concrete: "your dashboard tells you which three customers are about to
  churn."

When you find an abstraction in a headline, ask: *what does this look
like in the world?* Write that. Concrete writing is also harder to fake,
so it functions as a proof-of-thought signal — buyers can feel it.

## Psychology — the biases doing the work

Frameworks are surface structure. Underneath them, seven biases are doing
most of the persuasive work. Knowing which bias your framework is
recruiting lets you tune the line, and lets you avoid recruiting the
wrong one.

### Anchoring

**Mechanism:** The first number a reader sees colours every number that
follows. Anchors stick even when the reader knows they're being anchored.

**Tactics:**
- **Pricing pages:** lead with the highest tier (or an enterprise
  "Contact us" with no number). Mid-tier feels reasonable next to it.
- **Discount framing:** "Was $99, now $49" reads as a $50 win.
  "$49" alone reads as $49 spent.
- **Outcome anchors:** "Saved $2.3M in churn last quarter" anchors the
  scale of the result before the reader reads the next sentence.

**Failure mode:** Anchoring with a number the buyer immediately rejects
as fictional. An anchor only works if the reader half-believes it.

### Loss aversion

**Mechanism:** Losses sting 2–3× more than equivalent gains feel good
(Kahneman & Tversky, *Prospect Theory*). A buyer will work harder to
avoid losing $100 than to gain $100.

**Tactics:**
- **Expiration framing:** "Don't let your free trial expire" outperforms
  "Extend your trial" by margins that show up in A/B tests every time.
- **Status-quo cost:** PAS works because the agitate step makes inaction
  feel like a loss already underway.
- **Risk reversal:** "30-day money-back, no questions" lets the buyer
  reframe the purchase as not-a-loss.

**Lift:** McKinsey research finds loss-framed messaging lifts conversion
roughly **+21%** vs. gain-framed equivalents.

**Failure mode:** Loss framing ages worse than gain framing. It works on
the cold visit, but it fatigues. Use it for top-of-funnel where you need
the click; use gain framing for retention emails where the relationship
is what matters.

### Social proof

**Mechanism:** We do what we see others doing — especially others we
identify with. The closer the proof source is to the reader's
self-image, the stronger the signal.

**Tactics, in increasing strength:**
- Bare counts ("trusted by 10,000 teams") — weakest. Easy to fake.
- Logo bars — stronger. Visual, recognisable.
- Named testimonials with titles — stronger. "Ada Lovelace, CTO, Acme"
  beats "happy customer".
- Specific quote with a number in it — strongest. "We cut onboarding
  from 3 weeks to 4 days." — Ada.
- Live ticker / activity feed — strongest for time-bound urgency
  ("12 wallets minted in the last hour").

**Failure mode:** Proof at the wrong level. A solo founder doesn't care
that Fortune 500s use you. A Fortune 500 doesn't care that 47 solo
founders use you. Match the proof to the buyer.

### Authority

**Mechanism:** We extend trust to credentialed sources. Authority
transfers — naming a respected voice in the buyer's world borrows
credibility.

**Tactics:**
- Cite recognised research firms (Forrester, Gartner, NN/g) — useful for
  enterprise buyers who already use those names for cover.
- Quote named industry voices, not anonymous "experts".
- Credentials as breadcrumbs: "Built by ex-Stripe engineers" is
  authority-by-association; works when the association is recognisable
  to the buyer.

**Failure mode:** Borrowed authority that the buyer doesn't recognise
adds nothing and sometimes subtracts (it reads as namedropping).

### Reciprocity

**Mechanism:** We feel obligated to return value. A free, genuinely
useful thing creates a debt that buyers prefer to discharge by trying
the paid product.

**Tactics:**
- Free tools that solve a real micro-problem. The classic: HubSpot's
  Website Grader analysed roughly **2 million sites in its first 3
  years** and became a top-of-funnel engine.
- Calculators, scorecards, benchmark reports — anything that hands the
  buyer a number they didn't have before.
- Free tier that's actually useful, not a crippled demo.

**Failure mode:** The "free" thing is gated by a form before it's useful.
That's not reciprocity — that's a tollbooth. Reciprocity requires
delivering value *before* the ask.

### Scarcity

**Mechanism:** Limited supply = perceived value. Limited time = forced
decision. Both bypass deliberation by adding cost-of-waiting.

**Tactics:**
- Early-bird pricing with a real deadline.
- Cohort-based products (Y Combinator, ON DECK fellowships).
- Inventory counters ("3 seats left at this price") — only when real.

**Failure mode:** Manufactured scarcity. A "limited time offer" that
extends every Friday teaches the buyer to ignore deadlines. Scarcity is
a one-shot trust contract; spend it once dishonestly and it's gone.

### Decoy effect (asymmetric dominance)

**Mechanism:** Introducing a third option that's dominated by one of the
first two makes the dominant option look better than it would alone.

**Tactics:**
- Three-tier pricing pages. A "Basic" tier that's strictly worse than
  "Pro" on every dimension makes "Pro" feel like the rational choice.
- Bundles with a weak SKU added to make the main bundle obvious.

**Failure mode:** Three options that are all reasonable. The decoy has
to be visibly worse on the dimension the buyer cares about. If the buyer
has to think, the decoy isn't doing its job.

## How biases stack with frameworks

Frameworks recruit biases. Most pages recruit two or three. The strongest
pages recruit them in sequence so the buyer feels each one separately:

| Framework | Primary bias recruited | Secondary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| JTBD | Authority (you understand the job) | Social proof (others have the same job) |
| PAS | Loss aversion | Social proof (others feel this) |
| BAB | Loss aversion (Before) → reciprocity (you showed me the After) | — |
| Outcome + timeframe | Anchoring (the timeframe is the anchor) | — |
| Hard thing without pain | Loss aversion (the "without" is the loss removed) | Anchoring (the usual cost is the anchor) |
| Plain truth | Authority (confidence reads as competence) | — |
| Numbered nouns | Authority (designed system) | Anchoring (the numbers are the anchor) |
| Category for audience | Social proof (in-group identification) | Authority |
| Dunford positioning | Authority (you did the comparison) | Anchoring (the alternative is the anchor) |
| Real attributed quote | Authority + social proof | — |

If a page recruits no biases, it's a description, not copy. If it
recruits all seven, it's a circus. Pick the framework, name the one or
two biases it leans on, and tune the line until those biases land.

## Anti-patterns specific to copy frameworks

- **Mixing frameworks across the page.** PAS at the top, BAB in the
  middle, JTBD in the CTA — the buyer feels the seams. Pick one.
- **Framework without proof.** A template only works if you can fill it
  with specifics. JTBD without a specific job is just a fill-in-the-
  blanks ad.
- **Hype adjectives inside a framework.** "We help amazing teams ship
  incredibly fast without any friction at all" is JTBD with all the
  specifics replaced by hype. Strip the adjectives and you'll see the
  template was empty.
- **Stacking biases dishonestly.** Fake scarcity, fake social proof,
  fake authority — each one alone might convert short-term, all three
  together puts you in scammer territory. Buyers can smell it.
- **Loss framing on retention pages.** Loss aversion wins clicks; gain
  framing keeps customers. Don't tell a paying customer what they'll
  lose if they leave — tell them what they'll keep building if they
  stay.

## A working order

1. Pick the framework based on the buyer's state of mind (the three
   questions at the top).
2. Draft the headline in the template, with placeholders for the
   specifics.
3. Fill the placeholders with concrete nouns, real numbers, named
   audiences.
4. Run the Harry Dry test: could a competitor sign this? If yes, rewrite.
5. Name the bias the framework is recruiting. Tune the line until that
   bias is visible.
6. Read it aloud (see SKILL.md Step 6). If it stumbles, rewrite the
   rhythm without losing the specifics.
7. Ship. Measure. The framework that lifts conversion in your category
   is the framework you keep; the others are theory.
