# Part II — Speed and Conversion Stats (Primary Sources)

## Amazon — Greg Linden A/B tests (~2006)

> "In A/B tests, we tried delaying the page in increments of 100 milliseconds and found that even very small delays would result in substantial and costly drops in revenue."
> 
> — Greg Linden, Amazon (Stanford presentation, _Make Data Useful_, 2006) — every 100ms = ~1% in sales

## Google × Deloitte × Fifty-five — Milliseconds Make Millions (2020)

Based on 30 million user sessions across 37 brands in retail, travel, luxury, and lead generation.

> "A 0.1-second improvement in mobile site speed increased retail conversions by 8.4% and average order value by 9.2%."

> "A 0.1-second improvement in mobile site speed increased travel conversions by 10.1%."

> "A 0.1-second improvement drove a 40.1% increase in users moving from product detail to add-to-basket on luxury sites."

— Google × Deloitte × Fifty-five, _Milliseconds Make Millions_ (2020)

## Walmart — Cliff Crocker, Velocity Conference (2012)

> "For every 1 second of page-load improvement, conversions increased by 2%. For every 100ms, revenue increased by 1%."

> "Buyers loaded our pages in 3.22 seconds on average. Non-buyers loaded them in 6.03 seconds."

— Cliff Crocker, Walmart (Velocity Conference, 2012)

## Google — Mobile Page Speed Benchmarks (2017)

> "The probability of a mobile user bouncing increases 32% when page load goes from 1 to 3 seconds — and 123% at 10 seconds."
> 
> — Google, _Mobile Page Speed Industry Benchmarks_ (2017)

## Akamai — State of Online Retail Performance Report (2017)

> "A 100-millisecond delay in website load time can reduce conversion rates by 7%."

> "53% of mobile site visitors leave a page that takes longer than three seconds to load."

— Akamai, _State of Online Retail Performance Report_ (2017)

## Bing × Google Search — Velocity 2009

> "A 2-second slowdown changed queries-per-user by -1.8% and revenue-per-user by -4.3%."
> 
> — Eric Schurman, Bing (O'Reilly Velocity Conference, 2009)

> "A 400-millisecond delay resulted in a -0.59% change in searches per user. Even after the delay was removed, users still made -0.21% fewer searches — slower experiences change long-term behavior."
> 
> — Jake Brutlag, Google Search (Velocity Conference, 2009)

## Shopzilla — Phil Dixon, Velocity 2009

> "A 5-second speedup produced a 25% increase in page views, a 10% increase in revenue, a 50% reduction in hardware, and 120% more Google traffic."
> 
> — Phil Dixon, Shopzilla (Velocity Conference, 2009)

## Other industry case studies

# Part III — AI Speed and Latency

## TTFT — the AI equivalent of page load

> "For chat applications, TTFT under 1 second feels instant. Reasoning models often have high TTFT (10–150s) because they 'think' before responding."
> 
> — BenchLM.ai, _LLM Speed & Latency Comparison_ (2026)

> "A chatbot might require a TTFT under 500 milliseconds to feel responsive, while a code completion tool may need TTFT below 100 milliseconds for seamless developer experience."
> 
> — BentoML, _LLM Inference Handbook_ (2025)

> "In AI systems, users are more sensitive to the delay before the first token than to delays between subsequent tokens. The moment a user submits an input prompt, a silent countdown begins."
> 
> — IBM, _Time to First Token_ (2026)

> "Models below 50 tokens/sec feel sluggish in interactive applications. 200 tok/s produces roughly 150 words per second — fast enough for real-time streaming."
> 
> — BenchLM.ai (2026)

## AI conversion lift from speed

> "Brands using AI report a 3× lift in conversion rates and 50%+ growth in average order value through faster, intent-aware responses. A leading DTC skincare brand saw a 14% increase in checkout completion after optimizing latency."
> 
> — Alhena AI, _AI Latency in Ecommerce_ (2026)

> "Sub-second latency keeps users in a flow state, preventing decision fatigue. Every instant answer sustains trust and momentum."
> 
> — Alhena AI (2026)

## AI-native economics

> "AI-Native product gross margins benchmarks are 50–65%, while traditional SaaS benchmarks are 70–85%. The fastest growing AI-native companies — 'Super Novas' — typically have gross margins around 25% as they trade off gross profit for adoption."
> 
> — Bessemer, _State of AI 2025_

# Part VIII — Emergence, Swarm Intelligence, AGI, Superintelligence

## On emergence

> "Intelligence is not in the parts. It is in the pattern the parts make when they are forced to coordinate."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

> "More is different."
> 
> — Philip W. Anderson, Nobel laureate in physics (_Science_, 1972) — the founding sentence of emergence theory

> "Almost any clearly defined intellectual activity that can be done by a single human can also be done by a society of agents."
> 
> — Marvin Minsky, _The Society of Mind_ (1986)

> "The mind is what the brain does. And what the brain does is run a society of small, mindless processes that, together, are mindful."
> 
> — Marvin Minsky (paraphrased from _The Society of Mind_)

> "Complexity at the global level emerges from simplicity at the local level, with no central controller and no global plan."
> 
> — Eric Bonabeau, Marco Dorigo, and Guy Théraulaz, _Swarm Intelligence: From Natural to Artificial Systems_ (1999) — foundational text of ACO

## On ant colony optimisation and stigmergy

> "The coordination of activities in the colony does not depend on the workers themselves but on the nest. The work, in a sense, is done by the building."
> 
> — Pierre-Paul Grassé, on stigmergy (1959) — the principle ACO is built on

> "No single ant knows the solution. The colony does. And the colony is just the ants leaving notes for each other in the dirt."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

> "An ant carrying a crumb is following a pheromone trail laid by an ant who was following a pheromone trail. Somewhere down the chain, no one remembers why. But the food still arrives."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

> "Stigmergy is the substrate doing the thinking. The agents are just the parts that move."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

> "Ant colonies do not solve problems by being smart. They solve them by being many, being patient, and leaving signals the next ant can read. That is the entire recipe."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

## On the conditions for emergence

> "Life exists at the edge of chaos — not in order, not in noise, but in the narrow band between them where information can be both stored and transformed."
> 
> — Stuart Kauffman, _At Home in the Universe_ (1995), and Christopher Langton

> "You don't engineer intelligence. You engineer the conditions and let intelligence happen. The mistake of the last decade was trying to build the answer instead of the soil."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

> "Emergence needs three things: many small actors, a shared medium they can write on and read from, and feedback that rewards what works. Miss any of the three and you get noise instead of intelligence."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

> "From a small number of rules at the lowest level, an enormous amount of complexity can emerge. This is the key insight, and it changes everything."
> 
> — Stephen Wolfram, _A New Kind of Science_ (2002) — paraphrased

## On AGI and the path to it

> "Continual learning — the ability to learn in every new moment, as humans do — is one of the bottlenecks on the path to AGI."
> 
> — Demis Hassabis, CEO Google DeepMind (CNBC, January 2026)

> "The era of 'just add GPUs' is over."
> 
> — Ilya Sutskever, Safe Superintelligence (November 2025)

> "AGI will not arrive as a single model. It will arrive as a society of agents that have been learning from each other for long enough that we lose track of who taught what to whom."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

> "Every individual neuron is stupid. Every individual ant is stupid. Every individual agent will be stupid too. Intelligence has never been a property of the part. We keep forgetting this."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE

## On superintelligence

> "The first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control."
> 
> — I. J. Good, _Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine_ (1965) — the original superintelligence argument

> "Superintelligence will not be a thing we build. It will be a thing we notice, once a swarm of smaller intelligences has been talking to itself long enough that the conversation becomes more interesting than any single voice in it. Our job is not to build the mind. Our job is to lay the floor it walks on."
> 
> — Anthony O'Connell, Founder of ONE
