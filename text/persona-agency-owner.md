# persona-agency-owner.md — why an agency owner buys ONE

**Audience:** internal strategy doc. Drives the PDF, the deck, the website, the first-call script.
**Anchor:** Brad (PT Corp / BOQ) — a real, named, profile-fit agency owner we are already in business with. See `/Users/toc/Server/boq.md` and `web/boq.md` for the full picture. When the persona feels abstract, picture Brad reading it.
**Frameworks layered here:**
- **Jobs-to-be-Done** (Christensen, Klement, Moesta) — what the customer hires the product to do
- **Forces of Progress** (Moesta) — push of the current state, pull of the new, anxiety, attachment
- **StoryBrand** (Donald Miller) — hero / external + internal + philosophical problem / guide / plan / action / success / failure
- **April Dunford positioning** — competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit, market category
- **Value equation** (Hormozi) — dream outcome × perceived likelihood ÷ time × effort
- **Awareness ladder** (Eugene Schwartz) — unaware → most aware, and where Brad sits today
- **B2B emotional drivers** (CEB / Gartner) — career, social, identity, not just spreadsheet

The point of this doc is to never again write a sentence aimed at "agencies" in the abstract. Every paragraph is aimed at Brad. If it doesn't move Brad one notch, it's cut.

## 1. Who this is

**Brad** — owner of a marketing agency that sells to SMB local-service businesses. Mid-fifties. Built it over fifteen years from a one-man shop to a thirty-person operation. Currently selling marketing services (websites, ads, SEO, social) to dentists, accountants, restaurants, window-and-door installers, MDU developers, multi-location commercial — seven ICPs, 12,000 contacts on the list, 200-odd active client relationships. Average client pays low-four-figures a month for the agency's done-for-you marketing.

He has staff doing the fulfilment. He has churn. He has clients who don't pay on time. He has competitors. He has the same problem every agency owner has had for thirty years: **his agency's ceiling is his payroll.** Every new dollar of revenue costs him something close to a dollar of capacity.

He has seen what AI is doing to his business in two directions at once. Up: the big clients are asking why they're paying him so much when ChatGPT can write a blog post. Down: his juniors are writing copy with the same models, so he can't justify the rate any more. He knows he has two years, maybe three, to stop selling time and start selling something that scales.

He found us because his partner Donal kept telling him "this is the platform." He's spent a few hundred thousand on a forecast that says he can run 5,400 clients on this platform at $1k/month. He needs that forecast to be real. **That is the bet he is making.**

This is the person we are writing to. Not "agencies." Brad.

## 2. The jobs he is hiring ONE to do (JTBD)

Christensen's frame: *people don't buy products, they hire them to do a job.* For Brad, the jobs stack in four layers.

### Functional jobs

1. **Replace headcount cost with software cost.** Today he pays £80k/yr per junior copywriter. He wants to pay £80/yr per client for the same output.
2. **Ship 50 clients in 6 weeks without 50 onboarding meetings.** Pilot 1 is a load test on his time, not just his tech.
3. **Bill a flat monthly retainer that has 90%+ gross margin.** That's the unit economics that justifies everything else.
4. **Deliver the same five things to every client (chat, edits, leads, reports, payment) regardless of the client's industry.** Niche-pack the differences.
5. **Add a sixth and seventh service later (voice, social posting, in-channel commerce) without rebuilding anything.**

### Emotional jobs

6. **Stop being the bottleneck.** Right now every quote, every brief, every client escalation comes through Brad. He wants the agency to run without him.
7. **Stop dreading the talent meeting.** He doesn't want to lose another senior to OpenAI, Anthropic, or a competitor offering remote work and equity.
8. **Stop apologising for missed deliverables.** Most agencies are 70% on time. Software is 100% on time. He wants the apology call to go away.
9. **Stop being the guy who didn't see it coming.** Every founder his age is talking about AI. He doesn't want to be the laggard in his peer group.

### Social jobs

10. **Be the agency that "got AI right" in his peer network.** Win the award, give the keynote, write the LinkedIn post that gets a thousand likes from the people whose opinions he cares about.
11. **Show his clients he's modern.** SMB local-service operators are paying him for marketing expertise; they want the smartest person in the room, not the same advice they got in 2015.
12. **Become the person other agencies copy.** That's how he sells the agency in 2030 — by being a category-of-one.

### Identity jobs

13. **Stop being "an agency owner." Become a "platform founder."** Different multiples on exit. Different conversations at the dinner table. Different word on the gravestone.
14. **Leave something he can sell — or hand to his kids.** An agency's value is its clients; a platform's value is its IP and its data. He wants the latter.
15. **Be the founder who saw it, named it, did it.** Not the founder who waited.

The functional jobs are what he tells Donal. The emotional jobs are what he tells his wife. The social jobs are what he tells his peers. The identity jobs are what he tells himself when he can't sleep. **We have to land all four** to win him for real. The product page can hit the functional jobs; the founder-to-founder call has to hit the other three.

## 3. Forces of progress (Bob Moesta)

For any change, four forces are pulling on Brad at the same time. We win by *amplifying push and pull* and *defusing anxiety and attachment*. This is the cleanest single frame for the whole PDF.

### Push (what he hates about the status quo)
- Every win is a hire. The business doesn't scale, it just multiplies.
- Margins are flat or shrinking. Clients are renegotiating, AI tools are pushing prices down, juniors expect London salaries in Brisbane.
- Retention is a fight. He spent £4k acquiring the client; six months later they tell him the work is "fine, not amazing." That's the prelude to a churn call.
- Every report is a story he has to write. Every renewal is a story he has to defend.
- Talent leaves. The best person on his team got a job at a venture-backed competitor with stock options. He doesn't have stock options to give.

### Pull (what he wants in the new world)
- A platform that delivers the work, not a team that delivers the work.
- A repeatable 90% gross margin on every client.
- An agency that runs the way SaaS runs — once you sell it, the cost to serve doesn't scale with the price.
- A defensible business — corpus, IP, switching cost. Not "trust me, I'm good."
- A story for his next investor / next acquirer / next dinner party that doesn't start with "we're a digital marketing agency."

### Anxiety (what scares him about moving)
- *"What if I pick the wrong platform and they pivot, raise prices, or get acquired?"*
- *"What if my clients realise the AI is doing the work and demand I cut their bill?"*
- *"What if the AI screws up in front of a client and I lose them?"*
- *"What if my team feels replaced and quits the day I roll this out?"*
- *"What if my brand suffers because the bot says something weird?"*
- *"What if compliance / data / GDPR bites me?"*
- *"What if I spend the money and the tech still isn't ready?"*

### Attachment (what holds him in place)
- His agency's brand and reputation. Twenty years of goodwill.
- His team — some of them have been with him a decade.
- His client relationships, especially the ones he closed himself.
- His operating rhythm — Mondays are pipeline, Fridays are reports, that's how the business runs.
- The fact that his current model still pays his mortgage. Why blow it up?

**Use of this frame in copy:** every PDF page should name at least one push, one anxiety, and one pull, by the time the page ends. The "objections answered" block on each page is where anxiety lives. The hook line on each page is the pull. The opening paragraph is the push.

## 4. The hero's story (StoryBrand)

Donald Miller's seven beats. Brad is the hero. We are the guide.

### The Hero
A marketing-agency owner with a real business — staff, clients, revenue — and a real fear that the model he built it on is being commoditised in real time.

### The Problem
- **External:** his margins are compressing, his clients are asking AI questions, his juniors are using ChatGPT to do the work he's billing for.
- **Internal:** he feels he's lost the edge. He used to be the guy who saw three steps ahead. He doesn't feel that any more.
- **Philosophical:** an agency owner *should* own a business that runs without them. He doesn't. That's not just an inconvenience; it's a verdict on whether he built a business or just bought himself a high-paying, anxious job.

The philosophical problem is the one we win on. Every other agency platform sells against the external problem (margin, scale). We sell against the philosophical one: *you built this so you could own something — let's make sure you do.*

### The Guide
ONE. Not "we have features." Not "we have a 670-line runtime." We say: *we have shipped, we know agency operators, we have a real partner relationship with one of you already (Brad, by name, if it's right to drop), and we are choosing to operate as the substrate so you can own the customer.* Empathy + authority, in StoryBrand's words.

### The Plan
Three steps. That's all the customer can hold:
1. **See it work.** Open `/chat`. Watch one of the dream teams in action. Read the receipts.
2. **Buy 5,000,000 credits and start one client.** Pilot of one. Forty-eight hours from sign to live.
3. **Roll your book.** Move your existing clients onto the platform on a schedule that matches your churn rhythm.

We never make the plan more complex than this. Every "but what about" goes in the FAQ on the page that handles it.

### The Call to Action
*Deploy a Marketing, Sales and Service team to one client this week.*

Direct. One client. This week. Not "request a demo," not "talk to our team." A binary action with a measurable outcome.

### The Stakes — Failure
*Three years from now your agency is one of forty competing for the same SMB dentist budget, and the SMB dentist has built her own bot with the assistant her teenage daughter showed her.* Hit this beat once, in the cover or in the close, not twice.

### The Stakes — Success
*Three years from now your agency is the AI marketing platform for SMB local services in your region, the corpus of every client is the moat, and the multiple on exit has a different number of zeros.*

## 5. Positioning (April Dunford)

Five questions, answered for Brad explicitly.

### Competitive alternatives
What does Brad do today, or could plausibly do, instead of buying us?
1. **Hire more humans.** £80k a junior; takes a year to season.
2. **Buy a horizontal SaaS** (HubSpot, GoHighLevel, Vendasta). Pays the seat fee. Brand-locked to the vendor. Doesn't get smarter.
3. **Build it himself** on OpenAI / Anthropic / a frontend dev. 6-12 months of engineering risk. No substrate.
4. **Wait.** Hope the dust settles. Watch competitors get there first.
5. **Pick a generative-AI agency platform** (Jasper, Writer, etc.). Tool, not substrate. No multi-channel inbox. No agent learning.

### Unique attributes
What does ONE have that none of the alternatives have?
- **A signal substrate that captures every conversation across every channel into one record.** Not analytics — a substrate that *decides what to do next* based on what just happened.
- **Agents that improve themselves on the client's own data.** Not a chatbot that resets every session.
- **The 4-tier cascade** that makes white-label real — owner / agency / client / end-user, with the brand surface, OAuth screen, and invoice all flipping cleanly.
- **A credits-resold economics layer** that lets Brad set his own markup over a public floor.
- **Verb-surface parity** — every action is reachable from chat, CLI, IDE, MCP. Brad's team picks the surface.
- **Open substrate, closed product layer** — bus-factor protection without giving up the moat.

### Value (the themes those attributes enable)
Brad cares about three things. Order is the order.
1. **A business he can own** — corpus, switching cost, defensibility, multiple on exit.
2. **A business that runs without him** — fewer humans, more software, fewer Friday-night reports.
3. **A business that makes money** — 90%+ gross margin, agency markup on credits, performance-pricing optional.

### Best-fit customer (who buys this fastest)
- Agency owner, 30+ staff, multi-vertical client book
- Founder-led, with operational control
- Already losing margin to AI tooling competition
- Has at least one technically curious partner (Donal in Brad's case)
- Has $50k–$500k they can put behind a pilot
- Has a customer list big enough to roll fast (Brad: 12k contacts, 7 ICPs)

### Market category (the frame Brad uses to talk about us with his peers)
**"The substrate for AI-native agencies."** Not "another AI tool." Not "a chatbot platform." A substrate — the layer beneath what they sell. Their service stays *the* service. We're underneath, not in front.

When Brad introduces us at a dinner, the line is: *"It's the platform our agency runs on. We white-label it; the clients only see our brand."* That sentence should be the most natural thing in the world for him to say.

## 6. The value equation (Alex Hormozi)

`Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood) ÷ (Time delay × Effort/sacrifice)`

Pull every lever.

### Dream outcome (amplify)
Not "save time on writing copy." The dream outcome is:
- **5,400 SMB clients × $1k/mo = $5.4M ARR**, at 95% gross margin, with the corpus as a sellable asset, exit at 5–8× ARR.
- The agency that other agencies copy.
- A keynote at the next industry conference.
- The deal-of-the-decade in his city.

The cover page and §02 (Agency) have to *put numbers on this dream*. Not vibes — the actual £/$ math. Brad's deck already does this with the 5,400 number; we match the rigour.

### Perceived likelihood (amplify)
The whole PDF is a proof stack:
- **§10 Tracking** — proves the data is captured.
- **§13 Learning** — proves the loop closes.
- **§16 Speed** — proves the platform performs.
- **Receipts everywhere** — every claim drillable to evidence.
- **One named partner already in motion (Brad himself).** Social proof to the rest of the market; reassurance to Brad that we're committed.
- **Open-source substrate + escrowed product layer** — bus-factor handled.

### Time delay (compress)
- 60 seconds to a live chatbot.
- 48 hours to a fully-tuned client deployment.
- 6 weeks to a 50-client cohort.
- 90 seconds to ship the monthly client report.

Every one of these is a *time-to-value* lever. We name them on the cover, repeat them on §16 Speed, and remind on §02 (Agency).

### Effort/sacrifice (reduce)
- Zero engineering team required to start.
- One credit purchase, one credit pool, one markup setting.
- Edit an agent in markdown — no release cycle.
- One inbox, one record, one CRM — no integration sprawl.
- The substrate is open source; the agency keeps the corpus.

Every sentence that increases effort (long onboarding, training, integrations) erodes the equation. Every sentence that decreases it strengthens the buy decision.

## 7. Awareness ladder (Eugene Schwartz)

Schwartz's five levels. Knowing where Brad sits decides what we say first.

| Level | Description | Where Brad sits |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Unaware | Doesn't know they have the problem | Not Brad. He knows. |
| 2. Problem-aware | Knows the pain, hasn't named a solution | Brad as of 2024. |
| 3. Solution-aware | Knows a category of solution exists | Brad as of mid-2025. |
| 4. Product-aware | Knows ONE specifically, hasn't committed | Brad as of mid-2026 — where he is *right now*. |
| 5. Most aware | Committed, asking for the deal | Where we need him within 6 weeks. |

**Implication for the PDF and the deck.** The cover does not have to convince Brad the problem is real — he already knows. The cover has to *name our specific solution* and put the dream outcome and the proof stack in his hand on page one. The PDF is written for someone at level 4, moving to level 5.

For the broader market (other Brads), the website's homepage and the chat's first message have to do the level-3-to-4 work. The PDF is the level-4-to-5 instrument.

## 10. The objection ladder

The 12 objections in priority order. Every PDF page that lists "objections answered" pulls from this ladder.

1. *"What if you pivot or get acquired?"* — Open-source substrate. Product layer in escrow. The corpus is the agency's. Walk away with the data in one command.
2. *"What if my clients find out it's AI and demand a discount?"* — Most don't care. The ones who do are the ones we charge more. Tier 3 includes the "name the model" feature; tier 1 ships in your brand only.
3. *"What if the AI says something stupid in front of a client?"* — Voice contract enforced before each outbound message; quality score gate of 0.65. Below the gate, nothing ships.
4. *"What about GDPR / state AI laws / compliance?"* — Per-client data space, deletion in one command, named retention windows. Threat model is a table in §15.
5. *"Why not build it ourselves?"* — Six months minimum, an engineering team Brad doesn't have, and you'd still be on the wrong side of the substrate vs. tool divide. The economics don't work below ten clients.
6. *"Why not buy HubSpot / GoHighLevel / Vendasta?"* — Those are tools. The substrate is the layer underneath. They give you a CRM seat. We give you an inbox the customer is *in*. Different machine, different ceiling.
7. *"How do my existing tools fit in?"* — Inbound webhook in; outbound feed out. The substrate is additive. Older tools become reports.
8. *"What's the contract — can I leave?"* — Monthly or annual; portable export at any time; data and corpus stay with the agency.
9. *"What if I overcommit and burn through the credits?"* — Daily caps per workspace; auto-topup if you want it; soft fallback to Groq if you don't. The cost cap is mechanical.
10. *"What if my team feels replaced?"* — They become directors of teams of agents instead of doing the work themselves. One CMO running ten agents beats one CMO running one junior copywriter.
11. *"What's the worst case?"* — The platform delivers fewer than you hoped, you keep the data, you walk. You're not building on top of a closed substrate.
12. *"Why now?"* — Because the agencies that move now own the corpus. The corpus is the moat. Six months from now there are six platforms; eighteen months from now there are three; thirty-six months from now the corpus winners are uncatchable.

## 13. Sentences Brad should never read in this PDF

Anti-patterns specific to this persona. Any page that includes one of these fails the rubric.

- *"AI-powered."* (he is past this — patronising at his awareness level)
- *"Cutting-edge."* (banned in the voice contract anyway)
- *"Game-changer."* (banned)
- *"Reach out to learn more."* (he doesn't want to reach out, he wants to read on)
- *"In today's fast-paced world..."* (insult)
- *"Empower your team."* (he doesn't want to empower his team, he wants to need fewer of them)
- *"Built by AI experts."* (he's surrounded by AI experts; that's part of the problem)
- *"Future-proof."* (vacuous; he wants present-proof)
- *"Seamless integration."* (banned; also a lie about integrations anyway)
- *"Trusted by leading brands."* (no logos to show yet; don't claim what isn't there)
- *"Transform your agency."* (verb is overused and he's past needing the verb)
- Any unattributed adjectival praise. Every adjective needs a number.
